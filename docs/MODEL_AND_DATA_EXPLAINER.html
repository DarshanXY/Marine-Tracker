Section 1

The 60-second answer

If the panel asks one question and you have one minute, this is the whole story.

What the model is

A per-pixel neural network classifier — technically an ensemble of three small multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs). It looks at one 10 m × 10 m patch of sea at a time, reads 11 numbers describing the colour of that patch, and answers with one of four labels: Plastic, Algae, Sea Foam, or Water, plus a confidence score.

What it learned from

Real hand-labelled Sentinel-2 satellite data from published scientific benchmarks — MARIDA + MADOS (1,081,615 training pixels, 532,566 validation, 499,907 completely unseen test pixels) plus 3,789 points from four RFO scenes. Two independent exams are held back entirely: RFO's Durban and Marmara scenes, and every PLP deployed-plastic target.

How well it works

On the 499,907 unseen pixels: macro-F1 = 0.818. For plastic specifically, F1 = 0.799 (precision 0.776, recall 0.824). With our high-confidence filter switched on, plastic calls are 90 % precise — 9 out of 10 flagged pixels really are debris.

Say macro-F1, never accuracy. Our raw accuracy is 0.9899, which sounds spectacular and is actually meaningless: 98 % of ocean pixels are plain water, so a model that answered "water" to everything would already score ~0.98. Macro-F1 averages the score of all four classes equally, so rare-but-important plastic counts as much as water. 0.818 is the honest number.

What we built ourselves

The datasets are public, and PyTorch/NumPy/ONNX are libraries. Everything between them is ours: the four-class label mapping, the 11-feature spectral contract, the dataset readers, the loss function, the class-imbalance weighting, the decision-boundary calibration, the ensemble, the ONNX export with a bit-parity check, the two-tier confidence system, and the whole live inference path. Section 10 is the itemised list, file by file.

The whole journey on one line

raw satellite files (GeoTIFF) → dataset adapter → 11 features per pixel → .npz cache → GPU training (PyTorch) → ONNX export → live engine (onnxruntime) → map markers
Section 2

What the model actually does

Before the datasets and the training, be crystal clear about the job. It is a very narrow job, and that is deliberate.

One pixel in, one label out

A Sentinel-2 satellite image is a grid. Each cell of that grid — each pixel — covers a square of sea 10 metres across. The satellite does not record "colour" the way a phone camera does; it records reflectance in separate wavelength bands, including bands the human eye cannot see (near-infrared, short-wave infrared). Floating plastic reflects infrared light differently from water, and that difference is the entire physical basis of the project.

Our model takes the reflectance numbers for one pixel and outputs four scores that add up to 1 — how likely that pixel is Plastic, Algae, Sea Foam, or Water. The highest score wins.

INPUT (11 numbers for one pixel) OUTPUT (4 probabilities) B04 red reflectance Plastic 0.83 ← winner B06 red-edge Algae 0.09 B08 near-infrared Sea_Foam 0.05 B8A narrow near-infrared Water 0.03 B11 short-wave infrared FDI Floating Debris Index NDVI vegetation index PI Plastic Index MNDWI water index NDVI_RE red-edge vegetation BRIGHT overall brightness

Why four classes and not two

A naive "plastic vs. not-plastic" model floods the map with false alarms, because the two things that most look like plastic from space are floating algae/sargassum and sea foam. By forcing the model to name which of these it is seeing, we make it learn the differences instead of lumping every bright patch together. The four classes are our own mapping, condensed from the 15 fine-grained classes the source datasets ship with.

Why the last 4 features are free

Only the five band values (B04 B06 B08 B8A B11) are actually downloaded. The other six features are arithmetic on those five — published spectral indices that make the physics easier for a small network to learn. This matters practically: adding them cost us zero extra satellite data and zero extra download time.

The contract rule. These 11 numbers must be computed in exactly the same order and with exactly the same formulas during training and during live scanning — otherwise the model silently receives garbage. We enforce this by having one function in the trainer (augment11 in train_perpixel_gpu.py) mirrored line-for-line by one function in the engine (_features in inference_engine.py), with the order also frozen as a constant in model_loader.py. This is the single most common way an ML project quietly breaks, and we designed against it.

Why each adapter was real work, not boilerplate

DatasetThe problem in the raw filesWhat our adapter does about it
MARIDA Bands are stacked in one 11-band file; labels are 1–15 in a separate mask file; patch names encode their folder (11-6-18_16PCC_13 → S2_11-6-18_16PCC/). Resolves the filename convention, indexes out the 5 bands we need by position, and applies our 15→4 class map.
MADOS Every band is a separate file named by wavelength (..._rhorc_833_0.tif), stored in 10/, 20/, 60/ folders at three different pixel sizes. And the class IDs are renumbered vs. MARIDA. Sorts files by wavelength to recover MARIDA's band order, nearest-neighbour upsamples the 20 m and 60 m bands onto the 10 m grid, and — importantly — we verified the class renumbering empirically instead of copying MARIDA's map (see the note below).
RFO Six scenes from different years vary between 12-band and 13-band, L1C and L2A, and values are stored as integers (0–10000), not reflectance. Resolves band position per file via its embedded descriptions, divides by 10,000, and does one windowed read per labelled point. Splits by scene, not randomly.
PLP Targets are given as map coordinates in a shapefile with its own projection — not pixels. Some scenes are integer-scaled, some already reflectance. Reads the shapefile's .prj, builds a coordinate transformer into the scene's projection, converts to a row/column, and reads that one pixel. Decides the scaling factor once per scene from a whole-scene median.
A real bug we found and fixed — worth telling the panel. Our first PLP reader decided integer-vs-reflectance scaling per pixel. Dark water pixels read below the threshold even in an integer-scaled scene, so the divide-by-10,000 was skipped and raw integers landed in the cache — B11 ≈ 158, FDI ≈ −185, physically impossible values poisoning our hardest test set. Fix: decide the factor once per scene from a coarse whole-scene median, which cleanly separates the two cases. This is the kind of silent data bug that inflates or destroys results without any error message.
We did not trust the documentation. MADOS class 6 is documented as "Ships". It holds 140,000 pixels — far too many for ships. So we wrote a diagnostic (class_signatures()) that prints the mean spectral signature of every raw class. Class 6 came back with FDI 0.0117, NDVI −0.09 and water-like brightness: unambiguously water background, not ships. We mapped it to Water. Had we copied MARIDA's map blindly, 140,000 water pixels would have been mislabelled.
Section 5

Preprocessing, step by step

This is the pipeline that turns a folder of satellite GeoTIFFs into two NumPy arrays — x of shape [n, 11] and y of shape [n]. Nothing more exotic than that is needed, but every step has a reason.

Step 1 — Open the scene and its label mask

Read the reflectance array and the researcher-drawn class mask, and crop both to their common overlap (they occasionally differ by a row or column).

Step 2 — Put every dataset on one 10 m grid

Sentinel-2 records different bands at different resolutions (10 m, 20 m, 60 m). Our features mix bands across resolutions, so all bands are nearest-neighbour upsampled to 10 m. Nearest neighbour, not bilinear — interpolation would invent reflectance values that were never measured, and a fake bright pixel next to a real debris pixel is exactly the kind of false positive we are trying to eliminate.

Step 3 — Select 5 bands out of 11

We keep only B04, B06, B08, B8A, B11. Not to save space — because the live satellite pipeline only downloads these five. Training on bands we cannot fetch at run time would produce a model that is useless in production. The training contract is deliberately handcuffed to the operational contract.

Step 4 — Fix the scale to reflectance 0–1

Some providers ship integers 0–10,000; some ship reflectance 0–1. Everything is normalised to 0–1 so a pixel means the same thing regardless of which dataset it came from.

Step 5 — Compute the features

Apply the FDI and NDVI formulas → 7 base features. Later, in the trainer, apply augment11() → 11 features. Both stages are pure arithmetic on the five bands.

Step 6 — Map 15 research classes down to our 4

One mapping choice is our best anti-false-positive trick. Sending Sediment-Laden and Turbid Water → Water is deliberate. Muddy monsoon coastal water is bright and produces a high FDI, so a naive index-threshold detector screams "plastic" all along the Indian coast in monsoon season. By feeding thousands of labelled turbid-water pixels in as Water, we teach the network directly that bright brown water is not debris.

Step 7 — Drop non-finite pixels

Any pixel whose 7 features are not all finite (division by zero, nodata gaps, sensor artefacts) is dropped: np.isfinite(px).all(axis=1). One NaN reaching a gradient turns every weight in the network to NaN.

Step 8 — Respect the official splits

Both MARIDA and MADOS ship official train/val/test split files, and we use them verbatim rather than shuffling all pixels randomly. This matters enormously: neighbouring pixels in one scene are almost identical, so a random split leaks the test set into training and inflates every score. RFO is split by scene (train: Accra, Lagos, New Orleans, Venice — test: Durban, Marmara) and PLP by date (earlier dates train, latest 30 % test), for the same reason.

Step 9 — Cache to .npz

The result is saved compressed. This is not just a speed trick — it decouples the environments. Reading GeoTIFFs needs rasterio/GDAL; training needs a CUDA PyTorch build. Getting both into one Python environment on Windows is painful. Caching means the GPU trainer only ever loads .npz files and needs neither.

Step 10 — Standardise inside the model, not outside

Neural networks want inputs centred near zero. Rather than normalising in a preprocessing script the engine would then have to replicate exactly, we store the training mean and standard deviation as buffers inside the network and subtract them in forward(). When exported, the ONNX file therefore consumes raw features. There is no external normalisation step that could drift out of sync — a whole class of production bug is designed out of existence.

GeoTIFF → 10 m grid → 5 bands → reflectance 0-1 → 7 features → 15→4 labels → drop NaN → official split → .npz cache → augment11 → [n, 11] + [n]
Section 6

The model we trained

Architecture

Three independent multi-layer perceptrons, each with layer widths 11 → 256 → 128 → 64 → 32 → 4, ReLU activations and dropout between hidden layers. At inference all three run and their raw outputs (logits) are averaged before the winner is picked. About 141,000 parameters total; the exported file is 551 KB.

11 features │ ┌────┴────┬─────────┐ seed 1 seed 2 seed 3 ▼ ▼ ▼ 256-128-64-32 256-128-64-32 256-128-64-32 MLP #1 MLP #2 MLP #3 (identical shape, different random init) │ │ │ └────┬────┴─────────┘ ▼ average the 3 logit vectors ▼ softmax → Plastic / Algae / Sea_Foam / Water + confidence

Why an MLP and not a CNN — we tested it, we did not assume it

The obvious choice for satellite imagery is a convolutional network that sees spatial context. We built one — a residual ResNet-style CNN on 64×64×6 patches, GPU-trained (train_patch_cnn.py) — and evaluated it on the identical held-out test. It lost, badly and instructively:

ModelTrain accuracyHeld-out macro-F1Diagnosis
Patch CNN (ResNet, 64×64×6)0.98720.555 Memorised scenes. Huge train–test gap: with only a few hundred distinct acquisitions, 64×64 texture identifies the scene, not the plastic.

The CNN is kept in the repository behind an opt-in environment variable (CNN_MODEL_PATH) and its 0.555 is published in metrics.json. It is a reported negative result, not a hidden failure — and it is the single strongest evidence that our architecture choice was measured rather than guessed.

The second stage: Clay v1.5 as a verifier

We also wired in Clay v1.5, an open geospatial foundation model. Its encoder is frozen — we do not retrain it — and we trained only a small 1024 → 256 → 4 head on top of the 1024-dimensional embeddings it produces for a 64×64 chip.

Read that table carefully, because it explains our design. A pretrained backbone fixes the scene-overfitting that killed our own CNN and catches 2.4× more debris in seas it has never seen — but it is far more trigger-happy over open water, and its own plastic F1 is only 0.37. So Clay is not the detector. Our MLP detects; Clay confirms. When both agree that a patch is plastic, measured precision rises to 0.92. Two independent methods agreeing is a stronger claim than either alone.

Section 7

How training actually worked

Hardware: one NVIDIA RTX 4050 laptop GPU. Framework: PyTorch, in a separate conda environment (mpt-train) that is kept entirely out of the production engine.

The basic loop

  1. Load the cached .npz arrays — MARIDA + MADOS training splits plus the 3,789 RFO points from the four training scenes — and compute the 11 features.
  2. Compute the training mean/std and store them inside the model.
  3. For each epoch: shuffle, walk through in mini-batches of 8,192 pixels, forward pass, compute loss, backpropagate, step AdamW (learning rate 2×10⁻³, cosine decay, weight decay 10⁻⁴), with mixed-precision on the GPU.
  4. After every epoch, score macro-F1 on the validation split and keep a snapshot if it improved.
  5. At the end, restore the best-validation epoch — not the last one.
Point 4 and 5 are the honesty mechanism. Epoch selection uses macro-F1, not accuracy, on the validation split — never the test split. The 499,907 test pixels are touched exactly once, at the very end. Nothing about them influenced any choice.

The three techniques that beat the 1,134 : 1 imbalance

1. Focal loss written by us, 8 lines

Ordinary cross-entropy gives every pixel an equal vote, so a million easy water pixels drown out four thousand hard plastic ones. Focal loss (Lin et al. 2017) multiplies each pixel's loss by (1 − p_correct)^γ with γ = 1.5. A water pixel the model already gets right with 99 % confidence contributes almost nothing; a plastic pixel it is unsure about contributes almost fully. Attention is redirected to the hard, rare classes.

2. Class-balanced weights written by us

Each class is additionally weighted by the effective number of samples (Cui et al. 2019): w_c ∝ (1 − β) / (1 − β^n_c) with β = 0.999. This is deliberately gentler than plain inverse-frequency, and we chose it after being burned: full inverse-frequency weighting made an earlier model flag Sea Foam at 1.6 % precision — a flood of false positives.

3. Folded bias calibration written by us

Even a well-trained network's raw argmax is not the macro-F1-optimal decision boundary under extreme imbalance. So after training we run coordinate ascent over a small grid of per-class logit offsets, keeping whatever maximises validation macro-F1 — then add those offsets into the final layer's bias vector. The exported model needs no threshold logic at run time: plain argmax is already calibrated. This was the single largest macro-F1 lever we found.

4. Three seeds, averaged

Train the same architecture three times from different random initialisations and average the logits. Each network makes slightly different mistakes; averaging cancels the uncorrelated ones. Nothing is free — see the trade below.

The trade we made on purpose

A single calibrated 11-feature model actually scored a higher headline macro-F1 than the ensemble. We shipped the ensemble anyway:

MetricSingle model3-seed ensemble (shipped)Verdict

The reasoning is operational, not statistical. This system dispatches boats and crews. A false alarm sends a real crew to empty water and burns credibility; a slightly lower average score across four classes costs nothing operationally. We optimised for plastic precision and accepted 0.007 of macro-F1 to get four times fewer false alarms. That choice, and the fact that we can show the numbers on both sides of it, is the point.

Export, and proving the export is faithful

The trained PyTorch model is exported to ONNX (opset 13) so the production engine can run it with onnxruntime and never needs PyTorch installed — the engine is numpy + onnxruntime only. Export can silently change numerics, so we verify: push 256 random vectors through both PyTorch and the exported ONNX and compare predictions. Agreement = 1.0000, recorded in metrics.json. The thing we measured is exactly the thing that ships.

$ conda run -n mpt-train python -m ml.train_perpixel_gpu \ --epochs 80 --loss focal --weights cb --hidden 256,128,64,32 --calibrate

Nothing is promoted automatically. The trainer writes candidates (marinenet_pp7.onnx, marinenet_pp11.onnx) plus a head-to-head verdict against the currently shipped model on an identical test set. A human reads the verdict and copies the winner over marinenet.onnx. Every superseded model is kept (marinenet_pre_*.onnx) so any claim can be re-checked.

Section 9

The independent exams — including the one we fail

MARIDA and MADOS are the benchmarks we trained on, so good scores there are necessary but not sufficient. Durban and Marmara (RFO) and every PLP target were never trained on. Both results are published in-app, not buried.

Exam 1 — RFO: two seas the model has never seen

RFO's six scenes are split by scene, not randomly: Accra, Lagos, New Orleans and Venice contribute training points; Durban and Marmara are withheld entirely — 900 hand-refined points in two unfamiliar bodies of water. Measured live for this document:

MetricResultReading
False plastic over open water0.74 % The critical number. In two unfamiliar seas it stays calm: ~7 false alarms per 1,000 water points.
Debris recall30.7 % It finds under a third of the flagged debris. Honest and expected — see below.

Why recall drops from 82 % to 31 %: RFO's "debris" points are mixed floating matter — wood, sargassum and litter together — while we trained on "Marine Debris". Missing a floating log is arguably correct behaviour for a plastic detector. And crucially, the failure mode is conservative: it misses things rather than inventing them.

Why RFO's training scenes are in the model — measured, by ablation

We did not simply assume the extra data helped. We trained the identical ensemble recipe twice — same seeds, same configuration, RFO in versus RFO out — and scored both on the same held-out test:

Ensemble variantMacro-F1Plastic precisionPlastic recallPlastic F1
Without RFO training points0.7470.6940.9010.784
With RFO (shipped)0.8180.7760.8240.799

The mechanism is worth stating precisely: RFO's value is not more plastic examples — it is diverse global water negatives from six unrelated seas. Those sharpen the water/plastic boundary, which is why precision rises by 8 points while recall drops slightly. More negatives, not more positives, is what bought the reliability.

Historical note for completeness: an earlier, pre-ensemble single model that added RFO lost ground on the primary benchmark (0.787 → 0.767) and was rejected at the time — that experiment is still recorded in metrics.json under rfoExperiment. It was the later ensemble recipe, with focal loss and calibration, that turned RFO into a clear win. Both records are kept, so the decision trail is auditable rather than retrofitted.

Exam 2 — PLP: real plastic, physically deployed. We score 0 / 33.

Published, not hidden. The Plastic Litter Project floated real HDPE and PVC targets in the Aegean at known coordinates on known dates. On the date-disjoint held-out split — 33 pixels of genuinely verified plastic — our shipped model detects 0.

It also raised 0 false plastics on the 120 held-out water pixels from the same scenes, which tells you the model is not broken; it simply cannot see these targets.

Why, physically: the targets are 3.5–14 m across. A Sentinel-2 pixel is 10 m × 10 m = 100 m². A 28 m² target fills roughly a quarter of one pixel, so the pixel the satellite records is ~75–90 % water. The plastic signature is diluted below anything a spectral classifier can recover. This is a resolution limit of the sensor, not a defect in our training.

And we proved it is a resolution limit rather than a data gap, by testing the obvious objection — "just train on PLP too":

ModelPlastic precisionPLP targets foundDecision
Shipped (no PLP in training)0.7760 / 33Kept
Retrained with PLP included0.6602 / 33Rejected

Teaching the network that water-like spectra are "plastic" bought 2 extra sub-pixel targets and cost 12 points of plastic precision — thousands of new false positives across a real scan. We did not promote it. Reporting a tested negative result is stronger science than hiding it, and we would rather the panel hear it from us with the numbers than discover it themselves.

A looser variant of the same test — allowing a 3-pixel search window around each target across all 21 dates — does register a plastic hit on 7 of 21 dates (33 %). Both figures are recorded in metrics.json. We lead with the strict 0/33 because it is the less flattering one.

The ceiling, stated plainly

Plastic F1 cannot be pushed to 0.95 with Sentinel-2 spectral data, by us or by anyone. Plastic overlaps algae, foam and wet wood spectrally, and real debris is frequently sub-pixel at 10 m. The remaining levers are not better training recipes — they are higher-resolution imagery (PlanetScope 3 m, drones), SAR, or multi-temporal fusion: different projects, not tuning runs. So the honest claim is not "we detect plastic better than the field". It is: when we say HIGH-confidence plastic, we are right about 9 times out of 10, and we can show you exactly how we know that. We sell precision, and transparency about the limits.

Other limits we disclose in the app rather than in a footnote:

  • The drift forecast's physics harness exists (Liu–Weisberg skill score) but has not yet been validated against real drifter tracks, so ETAs are labelled as estimates.
  • The biofouling correction is a labelled heuristic, and is off by default.
  • Satellites pass every ~5 days and cannot see through cloud. Each pixel is 10 m, so only large patches are visible — never a single bottle. The app states this on its own landing page.

Section 11

How the model gets its data when the app is running

Training is offline and finished. This is what happens live when an analyst draws a box on the map and presses Scan — the same 11 features, computed by the same formulas, on fresh satellite pixels instead of cached ones.

STAGE 1
Find a scene
Query Copernicus for Sentinel-2 passes over the box; pick the least-cloudy composite under 50 % cloud.
STAGE 2
Download 5 bands
Fetch B04 B06 B08 B8A B11 at native 10 m resolution.
STAGE 3
Mask
Drop cloud, cirrus, shadow, snow, land and nodata pixels via the scene's own SCL layer.
STAGE 4
FDI screen
Compute FDI for every surviving pixel; keep only FDI > 0.02 as candidates.
STAGE 5
Classify
Build the 11 features per candidate → marinenet.onnx → class + confidence.
STAGE 6
Cluster
Group HIGH-tier plastic only into hotspots and score severity.
STAGE 7
Forecast
Push each hotspot 72 h forward on real currents + wind; raise alerts.
Why the FDI screen comes before the model. A modest scan area contains millions of pixels. Running a network on every one is wasteful, and the physics already tells us that debris must sit above the water baseline in the near-infrared. FDI is a cheap, published, interpretable filter that reduces millions of pixels to a handful of candidates; the network then does the genuinely hard part — deciding which kind of floating thing each candidate is. This is also why RFO/PLP recall measured through the full pipeline is lower than the model alone: a pixel has to clear the FDI gate before the model ever sees it.

Fail-loud design

If the ONNX file is missing or fails to load, the engine can fall back to a hand-written spectral rule set — but it logs an error, banners the UI, and tags the result "heuristic", and with REQUIRE_MODEL=1 it refuses to run at all. An un-benchmarked fallback must never be able to masquerade as a benchmarked model. Model loading is also behind a lock, so a second concurrent scan cannot catch a half-loaded state and silently degrade.

Reproduce any number in this document, live

# rebuild a dataset cache from raw GeoTIFFs python -m ml.datasets.marida python -m ml.datasets.mados python -m ml.datasets.rfo python -m ml.datasets.plp # retrain on the GPU (writes candidates + a head-to-head verdict, promotes nothing) conda run -n mpt-train python -m ml.train_perpixel_gpu \ --epochs 80 --loss focal --weights cb --hidden 256,128,64,32 --calibrate # independent exams python -m ml.validate_plp # deployed HDPE targets python -m ml.validate_plp_clay # + the Clay confirmation tier

The app itself carries an AI Lab page with an Evaluate button that re-scores the shipped ONNX model on a held-out split live, in front of the panel — macro-F1 first, accuracy captioned as water-inflated, confusion matrix drawn from the run. Nothing on that screen is a hard-coded number.

Section 12

Panel crib sheet

Short, honest answers to the questions most likely to be asked.

QuestionAnswer
Which model, in one sentence? A 3-seed ensemble of 11→256→128→64→32→4 per-pixel MLPs, trained with focal loss, class-balanced weights and folded bias calibration, exported to ONNX with proven parity.
What is it trained on? 1,081,615 hand-labelled Sentinel-2 pixels from MARIDA (2022) and MADOS (2024) official splits, plus 3,789 points from four RFO scenes — 1,085,404 in total. Then 532,566 validation and 499,907 untouched test pixels.
How many datasets? Five. MARIDA + MADOS + four RFO scenes train it; RFO's Durban and Marmara scenes and all of PLP are held-out exams; one synthetic baseline of our own that is never shipped.
How accurate? Macro-F1 0.818. Plastic F1 0.799. In the HIGH-confidence tier that drives dispatch, plastic precision is 90 %. Accuracy is 0.99 but that is water-inflated and we always say so.
Why not a CNN? We built one and it scored 0.555 versus 0.818 — it memorised scenes. The result is published in our model card, not hidden.
Isn't 0.99 accuracy suspicious? Yes, and we flag it ourselves. 98 % of pixels are water. That is precisely why we lead with macro-F1 everywhere in the product.
You detect 0 of 33 real deployed targets. Correct, and we publish it. They are 3.5–14 m targets in a 10 m pixel, so the pixel is ~90 % water. We tested the obvious fix and it made the product worse — plastic precision 0.776 → 0.660 for 2/33. On patch-scale benchmarks plastic recall is 90.6 % (MARIDA) and 89.9 % (MADOS).
How do we know you didn't tune on the test set? Epoch selection and calibration both use the validation split. The test split is read once, at the end. And you can re-run the evaluation live from the AI Lab page.
What did you actually build? Every line between the public datasets and the running product: the label mapping, four dataset adapters, the feature contract, the loss, the weighting, the calibration, the ensemble, the ONNX export and parity check, the two-tier confidence system, the Clay verifier, and the live inference path. Plus a from-scratch NumPy MLP with hand-written backpropagation and a hand-built ONNX graph, to demonstrate the fundamentals.
Biggest weakness? Sub-pixel plastic. It is a sensor resolution limit at 10 m, and the fix is higher-resolution imagery or SAR fusion — a different project, not a better training run.

Three rules when presenting this

  1. Lead with macro-F1 (0.818), and caption accuracy as water-inflated every single time.
  2. Never round upward. Say 0.82, not "about 0.9". Say 0/33, not "it struggles a bit".
  3. Volunteer the negative results — the 0.555 CNN, the 0/33 PLP, the unvalidated drift ETAs. Every one of them has a measured explanation and a tested alternative attached. Hearing them from you reads as rigour; having them discovered reads as a gap.
